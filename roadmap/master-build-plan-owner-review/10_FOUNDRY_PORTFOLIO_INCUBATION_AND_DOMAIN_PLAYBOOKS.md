# Command Center — Project Foundry — Portfolio Incubation & Domain Playbooks

Status: **OWNER-REVIEW ROADMAP ADDITION — RECONCILIATION REQUIRED BEFORE SPECIFICATION LOCK v2**

Purpose: support high-volume idea creation without overwhelming The Agency, and ensure “ultimate spec” means exhaustive for the actual kind of project rather than one generic software checklist.

This layer is especially important when James may introduce many ideas/projects in a short period. Command Center must capture all of them, develop them intelligently, prioritize them transparently, and let The Agency execute the right work without mixing project state or sacrificing quality.

## 48 explicit Portfolio Incubation & Domain Playbook controls

### A. Idea inbox and incubation — controls I1–I6

**I1. Unlimited logical idea inbox.** Ideas can be captured quickly without forcing immediate full project creation or execution.

**I2. Idea state model.** Ideas may be INBOX, RESEARCHING, INCUBATING, OWNER-APPROVED, PROJECT-CREATED, ACTIVE, PARKED, REJECTED, MERGED/RELATED, or ARCHIVED with provenance.

**I3. Duplicate/adjacency clustering.** Foundry detects ideas that solve the same problem, share infrastructure, belong to one product family, or should remain separate, without silently merging owner concepts.

**I4. Idea enrichment.** Brain may attach research, target user, problem statement, opportunity, risks, likely project type, dependencies, potential shared capabilities, and unknowns before project creation.

**I5. Idea parking without loss.** Low-priority ideas remain searchable and recoverable with original wording and research rather than disappearing because current worker capacity is busy.

**I6. Revisit triggers.** Parked ideas may surface again when owner priorities change, a blocker disappears, a relevant provider/capability becomes available, a related project finishes, or new evidence materially changes viability.

### B. Portfolio triage and prioritization — controls I7–I12

**I7. Multi-factor project scoring.** Authorized ideas/projects can be compared using owner-defined factors such as importance, urgency, time-to-value, revenue potential, personal value, strategic leverage, complexity, cost, dependency burden, risk, and required owner attention.

**I8. Score transparency.** Scores show their inputs/assumptions and never masquerade as objective truth; James can override priorities directly.

**I9. Capacity-aware activation.** Foundry may create many projects while Agency activates only the amount of concurrent work that resources, providers, verification capacity, and shared-resource safety can support.

**I10. Portfolio waves.** Related or high-priority projects can be grouped into execution waves/releases without combining their mutable project state.

**I11. Unlock leverage.** Shared-platform work that enables many projects can receive higher scheduling priority when consistent with owner priorities.

**I12. Opportunity-cost visibility.** When capacity is constrained, Command Center can explain what is delayed by choosing one major initiative over another rather than silently starving projects.

### C. Domain playbook registry — controls I13–I18

**I13. Project-type completeness playbooks.** Foundry maintains registered exhaustive-spec playbooks for different project classes rather than one universal checklist.

**I14. Composable playbooks.** One project may combine multiple playbooks—for example software + ecommerce + AI + media—without duplicating shared requirements.

**I15. Playbook versioning.** Projects record which playbook versions informed their specification; later improvements do not silently rewrite locked project scope.

**I16. Playbook extension contract.** New project types can register their own completeness matrix, lifecycle, specialists, risks, evidence, and acceptance gates without changing Foundry core.

**I17. Mandatory universal baseline.** Every playbook still inherits applicable universal controls for owner rules, security, privacy, data, evidence, recovery, extensibility, cost, and protected actions.

**I18. Domain exclusions.** Playbooks explicitly mark irrelevant controls N/A rather than forcing meaningless implementation merely to appear exhaustive.

### D. Initial domain playbook families — controls I19–I24

**I19. Software/product playbook.** Covers users/journeys, architecture, UI/UX, data, APIs, auth, environments, testing, observability, deployment, support, updates, migration, and scale.

**I20. AI/automation playbook.** Covers models/providers, grounding, evaluation, tool use, permissions, prompt/untrusted-input safety, cost/quotas, deterministic fallbacks, human boundaries, and provider replacement.

**I21. Commerce/digital-product playbook.** Covers products/catalog, suppliers or digital delivery, landed economics, pricing, storefront, payments, fulfillment/delivery, customer support, returns/refunds where relevant, marketing, analytics, and account isolation.

**I22. Service-business playbook.** Covers leads/customers, quotes, contracts, scheduling, service delivery, communications, payments, warranty/follow-up, assets, records, local operations, and protected customer data.

**I23. Books/content/media playbook.** Covers creative/product specification, protected assets, editorial/QC, rights/provenance, formats, metadata, publishing/distribution, rollout, content pipelines, analytics, and publication evidence.

**I24. Personal/internal-tool playbook.** Covers privacy, device behavior, convenience versus security, local/offline capability, household/family scope, data minimization, and absence of unnecessary SaaS/public-user complexity.

### E. Validation and stage-gate strategy — controls I25–I30

**I25. Ultimate spec does not mean blind maximum build.** Foundry may specify the complete future product while selecting an evidence-appropriate first build stage, preserving dormant/future architecture where needed.

**I26. Validation plan.** Projects with uncertain demand/behavior may define research, prototype, technical proof, user test, or market validation gates before expensive full implementation.

**I27. Technical-risk spike.** High-uncertainty architecture/provider/integration questions may receive bounded proof-of-concept work before the full build commits to that approach.

**I28. Evidence-based stage advancement.** Stage gates use defined evidence rather than optimism, elapsed time, or worker self-report.

**I29. Failure-to-validate response.** Weak validation may trigger redesign, narrower scope, park, or stop recommendation instead of automatically continuing because work already began.

**I30. Owner can override validation strategy.** James may explicitly choose to proceed despite uncertain validation, but the decision and risks are recorded rather than silently normalized.

### F. Outcome, feedback, and iteration loops — controls I31–I36

**I31. Project outcome metrics.** Applicable projects define real-world success measures beyond “build completed,” with source, window, and limitations.

**I32. User/customer/owner feedback intake.** Feedback can enter the project knowledge/roadmap with source and classification rather than becoming random patches.

**I33. Experiment registry.** Applicable products may run controlled experiments/A-B tests or alternative workflows with hypothesis, metric, scope, duration, guardrails, and evidence.

**I34. Outcome-to-roadmap loop.** Verified outcomes may create improvement proposals and roadmap changes without silently changing locked scope or protected assets.

**I35. Regression against original goal.** Post-launch analytics/reviews check whether optimization is improving the intended owner/user outcome rather than merely increasing a proxy metric.

**I36. Iteration history.** Major product generations, experiments, redesigns, and rationale remain traceable so the project does not rediscover already-failed approaches.

### G. Executive office and owner visibility — controls I37–I42

**I37. Executive portfolio brief.** Command Center can summarize what The Agency advanced, what changed, what completed, what is blocked, what needs James, and what is next across projects.

**I38. Daily/periodic brief capability.** Where James enables it, the executive brief can be produced on a schedule without requiring him to inspect every project individually.

**I39. Project launch radar.** Portfolio view can identify projects closest to launch/completion, projects stalled, and projects whose next owner action unlocks disproportionate progress.

**I40. Agency capacity view.** James can see active project lanes, queued work, idle/overloaded departments, major shared-resource bottlenecks, and verification backlog in understandable terms.

**I41. No activity theater.** Executive views emphasize verified outcomes, blockers, evidence, and next actions—not number of workers, tokens, messages, or animation as a substitute for progress.

**I42. Conversational executive control.** James can reprioritize, activate, park, or ask for deeper analysis of portfolio items through Brain without manually editing scheduler internals.

### H. Portfolio lifecycle and strategic reuse — controls I43–I48

**I43. Project-family relationships.** Command Graph can represent parent products, subsidiaries, spin-offs, shared platforms, book series, brands, companion tools, and related experiments without collapsing them into one project.

**I44. Shared-capability leverage registry.** Foundry identifies when building one reusable capability would reduce work across multiple authorized projects and proposes platform extraction when justified.

**I45. Portfolio archive and resurrection.** Inactive projects retain enough verified state/decisions/assets to restart later without carrying obsolete runtime assumptions forward.

**I46. Portfolio kill/merge/split workflow.** Projects can be intentionally closed, merged, or split with data/asset/decision lineage and dependency impact analysis.

**I47. Strategic lessons across projects.** Verified lessons can influence future project specs/routing while remaining scoped and provenance-backed rather than becoming uncontrolled global folklore.

**I48. High-volume creation acceptance test.** Final Foundry/Agency QA must demonstrate creation and safe management of a large synthetic portfolio, varied project types, mixed priorities, shared dependencies, and more logical projects than simultaneously active workers without context leakage or architecture degradation.
